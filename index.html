Job Market Paper

Beyond the Exploration–Exploitation Trade-off: The Behavioral Structure of Switching

Many sequential decision problems—research and development, investment projects, and contractual relationships—involve choosing between alternatives whose quality can only be learned through experience. Choosing an alternative of uncertain quality provides information about its underlying quality, but at the cost of less predictable payoffs relative to more familiar alternatives. A common normative benchmark for how agents switch between alternatives is that switching decisions respond to new information about their relative qualities. I take a step back and ask whether new information about alternative quality is necessary for a switching decision. Using an incentivized one-armed bandit experiment—a repeated choice between a known-quality option and a quality-uncertain one—I find that it is not. More than half of subjects deviate from the benchmark by switching even when outcomes provide no new information about the relative quality of the alternatives. These subjects choose the uncertain alternative more often and earn 25% less than subjects who follow the normative benchmark. Consistent with this, when these subjects are constrained to use the normative switching strategy, they choose the uncertain alternative less often. I use additional experimental variation to distinguish between types of deviations from the benchmark. One type attenuates or disappears when subjects are encouraged to evaluate decisions over multiple rounds rather than react to individual outcomes, consistent with experience-driven deviations, arising from the experience of play. A second persists even when subjects neither execute the choice strategy themselves nor observe round-by-round outcomes, suggesting valuation-driven deviations, reflecting differences in how subjects evaluate the alternatives themselves.

Working Paper

Legal Information as an Input to Health Risk Assessments: Evidence from E-Cigarettes in Latin America

with José Antonio Márquez, Fernando Briseño, and Víctor Pérez

When the health risks of an activity are uncertain, individuals may use its legal status as a signal when assessing those risks. We use a randomized survey experiment in Mexico, Argentina, and Chile to test whether information about e-cigarette regulation causes individuals to update their perceptions of health risk.
